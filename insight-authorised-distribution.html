What “Authorised Distribution” Actually Means (And Why It Matters)

A short guide for trade buyers weighing up where their stock actually comes from.
Back to Insights

Two boxes of the same product can look identical and still have come from completely different places in the supply chain. One might be "authorised" stock, sourced through a formal agreement with the brand. The other might be "grey market": stock that's genuine, but has moved through channels the brand never sanctioned. To a buyer glancing at a pallet, there's often no visible difference. In practice, the difference is significant.

What "authorised" actually means

An authorised distributor has a direct commercial relationship with the brand or manufacturer: a formal agreement covering what can be sold, where, at what pricing, and under what conditions. The brand knows exactly where its stock is going and has some say over how it's represented downstream.

Grey market stock, by contrast, is genuine product that's been diverted from its intended market. It might have been bought up cheaply in one region and shipped into another, bulk-bought and resold outside agreed channels, or moved on by a retailer who was never meant to wholesale it in the first place. Nothing about the product itself is fake, but the brand has lost visibility and control over where it ends up.

Why brands care so much about this

Pricing integrity is the obvious one: if grey market stock floods a market at a lower price point, it undercuts the retailers who agreed to sell at RRP, which damages the brand's relationships with its legitimate network. But it goes further than pricing. Brands lose the ability to guarantee batch traceability, proper storage and handling, and consistent presentation. Warranty terms can be affected too: some manufacturers will refuse to honour a warranty on stock that didn't move through an authorised channel, since they have no record of it.

Why it should matter to a trade buyer, not just the brand

It's tempting to think this is a brand's problem to worry about, not a buyer's. In practice, it becomes a buyer's problem quickly:

  • Supply consistency. Grey market channels can dry up without warning: there's no ongoing relationship underpinning them, just an opportunistic one-off source.
  • No comeback if something's wrong. If stock turns out to be near end-of-life, mislabelled, or otherwise problematic, there's rarely a real point of contact to resolve it with.
  • Reputational risk. If a brand identifies its stock circulating through unauthorised channels, buyers further down that chain can find themselves cut off from future supply entirely, including from that brand's authorised distributors.

How to tell the difference

In practice, it comes down to asking direct questions of whoever you're buying from: can they name the specific agreement or relationship the stock came through? Can they provide proper invoicing that traces back to an authorised source, rather than a vague "direct from supplier" claim? A distributor confident in its sourcing will answer both without hesitation.

This is the whole reason authorised-only sourcing sits at the centre of how we operate. Every line we carry is backed by a direct, formal relationship with the brand or manufacturer, not picked up opportunistically wherever it's cheapest. It's slower to build a catalogue this way, but it means every trade partner we work with can trust exactly where their stock has come from.
